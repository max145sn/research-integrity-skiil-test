steosarcoma is the most common primary malignant 
tumour originating from bone in adolescents and chil-
dren. While patients with non-metastatic diagnosis have a 
60–70% 5-yr-survival rate, this number drops to 20% for metastatic 
cases1–5. Osteosarcoma is characterized by marked instability of its 
somatic genome, which frequently features chromothripsis, chro-
mosomal aneuploidy and chromosomal rearrangements6–12. The 
structural variations produced by frequent chromosomal rearrange-
ments contribute to the majority of genetic lesions in osteosarcoma, 
which lead to the production of truncated or fusion proteins with 
varying functions and inactivation of many key tumour suppres-
sor genes including TP536,13 and ATRX7,9,14,15. There are no therapies 
for osteosarcoma targeting truncated or fusion proteins, although 
many fusion proteins have been successfully used as therapeutic tar-
gets for other cancer types in the clinic16–19.
Prevailing algorithms that search RNA sequences for struc-
tural variations filter out intron-related structural variations. This 
means that very few fusions involving long non-coding RNAs have 
been reported and that most of the reported transcripts derived 
from structural variation-induced fusion from cancers including 
osteosarcoma are derived from exons only20,21. Nevertheless, chro-
mosomal rearrangements can result in fusion proteins with the 
potential to promote metastasis22. Owing to variation in recombina-
tion sites, variations including exon–exon and exon–intron fusions 
can occur on a single gene, with complex consequences for disease 
progression. To comprehensively investigate structural variations 
associated with metastasis of osteosarcoma, we used TopHat-fusion 
and SQUID algorithms that preserve exon–intron fusion informa-
tion. We identified exon–intron fusion genes that juxtapose amino 
acids 1–38 of the trafficking-related Rab22a protein (Rab22a1–38) 
with inverted introns and untranslated regions of chromosome 20 
in multiple osteosarcoma cell lines and clinical samples. We des-
ignated the fusion proteins encoded by these transcripts—which 
promote osteosarcoma lung metastasis—as Rab22a-NeoFs. The 
Rab22a1–38 moiety exhibits constitutive binding to SmgGDS-607 
to activate RhoA and is sufficient to promote osteosarcoma lung 
metastasis. Rab22a-NeoF1-mediated lung metastasis was abrogated 
by the tumour-homing and cell-penetrating peptide iRGD, which 
targets the interaction with SmgGDS-607.

these transcripts contain the first two exons of RAB22A, which 
encode the first 38 amino acids of Rab22a and multiple inverted

Osteosarcoma is a type of aggressive malignant bone tumour that frequently metastasizes to lungs, resulting in poor progno-
sis. However, the molecular mechanisms of lung metastasis of osteosarcoma remain poorly understood. Here we identify exon–
intron fusion genes in osteosarcoma cell lines and tissues. These fusion genes are derived from chromosomal translocations 
that juxtapose the coding region for amino acids 1–38 of Rab22a (Rab22a1–38) with multiple inverted introns and untranslated 
regions of chromosome 20. The resulting translation products, designated Rab22a-NeoFs, acquire the ability to drive lung 
metastasis of osteosarcoma. The Rab22a1–38 moiety governs the function of Rab22a-NeoFs by binding to SmgGDS-607, a GTP–
GDP exchange factor of RhoA. This association facilitates the release of GTP-bound RhoA from SmgGDS-607, which induces 
increased activity of RhoA and promotes metastasis. Disrupting the interaction between Rab22a-NeoF1 and SmgGDS-607 with 
a synthetic peptide prevents lung metastasis in an orthotopic model of osteosarcoma. Our findings may provide a promising 
strategy for a subset of osteosarcoma patients with lung metastases.

Notably, 2 out of 37 (5.4%) osteosarcoma samples from patients

RAB22A-NeoF-negative patients with metastasis had a median sur-
vival time of 19 months (Supplementary Table 2).
Since Rab22a-NeoF1–6 all contain Rab22a1–38, this suggests that 
residues 1–38 of Rab22a are crucial for their functions. Indeed, 
when stably expressed in 143B or U2OS/MTX300 cells, Rab22a1–38, 
Rab22a1–38 conjugated to green fluorescent protein (Rab22a1–38–
GFP) and Rab22a-NeoF1 (the longest fusion transcript) promoted 
cell migration and invasion, but not cell proliferation (Fig. 1d 
and Extended Data Fig. 1g). Furthermore, using these stable cell 
lines in an in vivo orthotopic model of osteosarcoma metastasis, 
Rab22a-NeoF1, Rab22a1–38 and Rab22a1–38–GFP promoted lung 
metastases, as indicated by increases in wet lung weight and the 
number of metastatic nodules in lungs (Fig. 1e–g and Extended Data 
Fig. 1h) and decreased the survival time of mice (Extended Data 
Fig. 1i,j). Similarly, Rab22a-NeoF2, Rab22a-NeoF3, Rab22a-NeoF4, 
Rab22a-NeoF5 and Rab22a-NeoF6 were able to drive lung metasta-
ses of 143B cells stably expressing these fusion genes in this ortho-
topic model of metastasis (Fig. 2a–d). These results demonstrate 
that Rab22a1–38 determines the functions of Rab22a-NeoF1–6 and 
drives osteosarcoma lung metastasis.
Next, we investigated whether similar fusions exist in other 
cancer types. We identified two cases of breast cancer with 
fusions involving RAB22A in the cBio data portal, one of which 
has Rab22a1–38 fused to neurogenic differentiation factor 2 (neu-
roD2) (Supplementary Table 3). A search of the Cancer Cell Line 
Encyclopedia (CCLE) RNA-seq dataset showed that the BT-474 
breast cancer cell line and HCC2935 lung cancer cell line contain 
fusions of RAB22A1–38 with MYO9B and TEF, respectively (hereafter 
referred to as RAB22A-MYO9B and RAB22A-TEF; Supplementary 
Table 4); this was further validated by RT–PCR and Sanger sequenc-
ing (Fig. 2e). Using specific short interfering RNAs (siRNAs) or 
short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting the RAB22A-MYO9B 
and RAB22A-TEF sequences to knock down these fusion genes in 
BT-474 cells and HCC2935 cells, respectively (Fig. 2f–i), reduced 
cell migration and invasion (Fig. 2j). Lung metastases of BT-474 cells 
were also decreased when RAB22A-MYO9B was knocked down in 
a tail-vein-injection model (Fig. 2k–m). These results indicate that 
fusion genes that encode Rab22a1–38 occur and drive metastasis in 
multiple cancer types.
RAB22A-NeoF1 is required for lung metastasis of osteosarcoma. 
Overexpression of the six Rab22a-NeoFs in U2OS cells revealed 
that the protein products of RAB22A-NeoF1 and RAB22A-NeoF5 
were more stable than those of RAB22A-NeoF2, RAB22A-NeoF3, 
RAB22A-NeoF4 and RAB22A-NeoF6, which were more eas-
ily degraded by the proteasome and/or lysosome (Extended Data 
Fig. 2a). As RAB22A-NeoF1 and RAB22A-NeoF2 accounted for the 
majority of reads of RAB22A-NeoF1–6 transcripts (Extended Data 
Fig. 2b), the functions of endogenous RAB22A-NeoFs are likely to 
be dominated by RAB22A-NeoF1 in both ZOS and ZOS-M cells, 
although endogenous Rab22a-NeoF2–6 proteins were also detect-
able in both cell types (Extended Data Fig. 2c). Therefore, we 
selected RAB22A-NeoF1 for further investigation in this study.
We amplified full-length RAB22A-NeoF1 by 3′ and 5′ rapid 
amplification of complementary DNA ends. It comprises the cod-
ing sequence for Rab22a1–38 and inverted intron sequences from 
between exon 1 and exon 2 of DOK5. The predicted open read-
ing frame corresponds to a coding sequence for 186 amino acids 
(Extended Data Fig. 2d). The presence of the RAB22A-NeoF1 tran-
script was further confirmed by fluorescence in situ hybridization 
(FISH), showing that it is specific to osteosarcoma tissues and ZOS 
and ZOS-M cells (Fig. 3a and Extended Data Fig. 2e,f). To examine 
endogenous Rab22a-NeoF1 in cell lines and tumour tissues, we gen-
erated a Rab22a-NeoF1-specific monoclonal antibody (RAD5-8) 
and a humanized version (hRAD5-8-v1-R5) using the sequence 
68QQKSTTPASNHGKSR82 as epitope (Extended Data Fig. 3a). Using 
ZOS-M cells as a positive control, we showed that hRAD5-8-v1-R5 
could detect endogenous Rab22a-NeoF1 immunoprecipitated by 
RAD5-8 antibody from RAB22A-NeoF1-positive T2 tumour tis-
sues, but not from RAB22A-NeoF1-negative T3 tumour tissues, as 
indicated by RT–PCR (Figs. 1b and 3b), although the signal was 
relatively low, probably because the RAB22A-NeoF1-positive tis-
sues made up a small proportion of the tumour tissues, as indi-
cated by FISH (Fig. 3a). We also designed two siRNAs targeting 
the inverted intron sequence of DOK5, which efficiently knocked 
down RAB22A-NeoF1 expression without affecting mRNA levels of 
RAB22A or DOK5 (Extended Data Fig. 3b). Knockdown of endog-
enous Rab22a-NeoF1 inhibited cell migration and invasion in both 
ZOS and ZOS-M cells; this effect was completely rescued by the 
reintroduction of Rab22a-NeoF1 (Fig. 3c,d and Extended Data Fig.

Rab22a-NeoF1 promotes lung metastasis by activating RhoA. 
To investigate how Rab22a-NeoF1 drives metastasis, we per-
formed tandem affinity purification (TAP) with mass spectrom-
etry (TAP–MS) to explore the Rab22a-NeoF1 protein interactome. 
We identified Rho family members that have key roles in metas-
tasis—such as RhoA, Cdc42 and Rac130,31—in the TAP–MS data 
(Extended Data Fig. 4a and Supplementary Table 5). To confirm 
the interaction between Rab22a-NeoF1 and Rho family members 
(RhoA, RhoB, RhoC, Rac1 and Cdc42), we performed coimmuno-
precipitation using ectopically expressed proteins. Rab22a-NeoF1 
coimmunoprecipitated with RhoA, RhoB and RhoC but not 
with Rac1 or Cdc42, whereas wild-type Rab22a did not coim-
munoprecipitate with any Rho family members (Extended Data 
Fig. 4b,c). Ectopic expression of Rab22a-NeoF1 also increased 
the amount of GTP-bound RhoA (RhoA–GTP; the active form of 
RhoA) and membrane-associated RhoA, but had no such effect 
on RhoB or RhoC (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 4d). By con-
trast, knockdown of Rab22a-NeoF1 decreased both RhoA–GTP 
and membrane-associated RhoA (Fig. 4b). In addition, complexes 
containing Rab22a-NeoF1 and RhoA were detectable at both 
ectopic and endogenous levels of Rab22a-NeoF1 (Extended Data 
Fig. 4e,f). These results indicate that RhoA is a key functional part-
ner of Rab22a-NeoF1.
The enhancement of cell migration, invasion and adhesion by 
ectopic Rab22a-NeoF1 was abrogated by treatment with short hair-
pin RNAs targeting RhoA (shRhoA #1 and shRhoA #2) or with 
the RhoA inhibitor CT04 (Fig. 4c,d and Extended Data Fig. 4g–k). 
Knockout of RhoA using clustered regularly interspaced short pal-
indromic repeats (CRISPR)–CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9) 
with the single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) sgRhoA #1 and sgRhoA #2 
abolished Rab22a-NeoF1-induced enhancement of cell migration, 
invasion and adhesion; these effects were rescued when RhoA was 
reintroduced into these cells (Extended Data Fig. 5a–c). The impair-
ment in cell migration, invasion and adhesion caused by knockdown 
of Rab22a-NeoF1 was rescued by ectopic expression of RhoA in the 
cells (Fig. 4e,f). Furthermore, in both the orthotopic osteosarcoma 
metastasis model and the tail-vein-injection metastasis model, the 
promotion of lung metastases by ectopic Rab22a-NeoF1 was abro-
gated by shRhoA #1 and shRhoA #2, and knockdown of RhoA pro-
longed the survival of mice induced by Rab22a-NeoF1 (Fig. 4g–j 
and Extended Data Fig. 5d–g). Together, these results suggest that 
Rab22a-NeoF1 promotes osteosarcoma lung metastasis by activat-
ing RhoA.
Rab22a-NeoF1 constitutively binds to a negatively charged region 
of SmgGDS-607. We further mined the TAP–MS data for infor-
mation on how RhoA is activated by Rab22a-NeoF1. We turned 
our attention to RAP1GDS1 (also known as SmgGDS and GDS1), 
a specific non-canonical guanine exchange factor for RhoA and 
RhoC32 (Extended Data Fig. 4a). We co-transfected two isoforms 
of SmgGDS, SmgGDS-558 and SmgGDS-607, with either Rab22a 
or Rab22a-NeoF1 tagged with S protein–Flag–streptavidin-binding 
peptide (SFB). Rab22a-NeoF1 exhibited stronger binding than 
Rab22a to both SmgGDS-558 and SmgGDS-607, with a preference 
for SmgGDS-607 (Fig. 5a). SmgGDS-607 was more highly expressed 
than SmgGDS-558 in osteosarcoma cell lines (Extended Data Fig. 
6a,b). The interaction between Rab22a-NeoF1 and SmgGDS-607 
was detectable with both ectopic and endogenously expressed pro-
teins (Fig. 5a and Extended Data Fig. 6c,d).
A negatively charged region of SmgGDS is reported to be crucial 
for its association with RhoA, as the E254K/D256K double mutant 
of SmgGDS-607 diminishes its RhoA-binding ability32–34. This 
region of SmgGDS is also important for the interaction between 
Rab22a-NeoF1 and SmgGDS, as SmgGDS(E254K/D256K) failed to 
bind Rab22a-NeoF1 (Fig. 5b). Moreover, the interaction between 
SmgGDS-607 and RhoA was notably diminished in the presence of 
Rab22a-NeoF1 (Fig. 5c). These results indicate that Rab22a-NeoF1 
influences the binding of SmgGDS-607 to RhoA.
Knockout of SmgGDS using CRISPR–Cas9 and sgRNAs sgSmg-
GDS #1 and sgSmgGDS #2 abolished the increased cell migra-
tion, invasion and adhesion induced by Rab22a-NeoF1; these 
effects were rescued by reintroduction of wild-type SmgGDS, 
but not SmgGDS(E254K/D256K), into these cells (Fig. 5d,e and 
Extended Data Fig. 6e,f). Furthermore, in the orthotopic osteo-
sarcoma metastasis model, the promotion of lung metastases by 
ectopic Rab22a-NeoF1 was abrogated by sgSmgGDS #1 and sgSmg-
GDS #2, and knockout of SmgGDS prolonged the survival of mice 
bearing tumours overexpressing Rab22a-NeoF1. These pheno-
types were completely restored when wild-type SmgGDS, but not 
SmgGDS(E254K/D256K), was reintroduced into these cells (Fig. 
5f–i and Extended Data Fig. 6g–j). In addition, adhesion, migra-
tion, invasion and lung metastases were impaired by knocking 
down SmgGDS-607 in ZOS-M cells with endogenous expression of 
Rab22a-NeoF1 (Extended Data Fig. 7a–g). These results suggest that 
the association with SmgGDS-607 is required for Rab22a-NeoF1 to 
promote osteosarcoma lung metastasis.

(Fig. 6b and Extended Data Fig. 9a). In the orthotopic osteosarcoma 
metastasis model, amino acids 1–10 of Rab22a-NeoF1 was essential 
for the promotion of lung metastases (Fig. 6c–e and Extended Data 
Fig. 9b), and deletion of these residues from Rab22a-NeoF1 pro-
longed the survival of these mice (Extended Data Fig. 9c,d).
Amino acids 1–10 of Rab22a-NeoF1 include the positively 
charged residues Arg4 and Lys7. As this region binds to the negatively 
charged region of SmgGDS-607, we tested whether Rab22a-NeoF1 
Arg4 and Lys7 were required for this interaction. In the structure 
of full-length Rab22a35, Lys7 is blocked by the main-chain oxygen 
atoms of Met73, Tyr74 and Arg76, whereas Arg4 is surface-exposed 
(Extended Data Fig. 9e). In Rab22a-NeoF1, however, Arg4 and Lys7 
are potentially exposed because of the truncation and possible dif-
ferences in folding from that of wild-type Rab22a. We mutated Arg4 
and Lys7 to Asp or Glu in Rab22a-NeoF1 (R4D/K7D, hereafter DD; 
R4E/K7E, hereafter EE). As expected, both DD and EE mutants 
of Rab22a-NeoF1 lost the ability to bind to SmgGDS-607 in cells 
(Fig. 6f). Furthermore, purified SmgGDS-607–His was pulled 
down by Rab22a-NeoF1, but not by the DD mutant (Extended Data 
Fig. 9f). Similar to the deletion of Rab22a residues 1–10, none of 
the vivo or in vitro phenotypes of Rab22a-NeoF1, such as status of 
RhoA, cell migration, invasion, adhesion, lung metastases in mice 
and survival, were observed with the DD mutant (Fig. 6g–k and 
Extended Data Fig. 9g–j). Thus, Arg4 and Lys7 of Rab22a-NeoF1 
are required for its binding to SmgGDS-607 to promote lung 
metastasis, confirming that the interaction of Rab22a-NeoF1 with 
SmgGDS-607 is required for Rab22a-NeoF1 to execute its functions.
The interaction between Rab22a-NeoF1 and SmgGDS-607 is a 
potential therapeutic target for osteosarcoma lung metastasis. We 
synthesized peptides based on amino acids 1–10 of Rab22a-NeoF1 
in an attempt to block the functions of Rab22a-NeoF1, as shown in 
Fig. 7a. The biotin-labelled peptide (biotin–MALRELKVCL, bio-
tin–pep(WT)), but not the DD mutant (biotin–MALDELDVCL, 
biotin–pep(DD)), specifically bound to SmgGDS from cell lysates 
of both ZOS and ZOS-M cells that were positive for Rab22a-NeoF1 
in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 7b and Extended Data 
Fig. 10a). Moreover, RhoA–GTP levels increased after whole-cell 
lysates from 143B cells were incubated in  vitro with either 
amino acids 1–38 of Rab22a or Rab22a-NeoF1(WT), but not 
with their DD mutants, and this effect was abolished by addi-
tion of MALRELKVCL (peptide(WT)), but not MALDELDVCL 
(peptide(DD)) (Fig. 7a,c,d). These results also indicated that the 
interaction between residues 1–10 of Rab22a-NeoF1 (for example, 
peptide(WT)) and SmgGDS-607 is insufficient to activate RhoA; 
that is, residues among amino acids 11–38 of Rab22a-NeoF1 are 
also required to activate RhoA. Consistent with these results, 
mutation of Lys18, a conserved residue, to either Asp or Glu did 
not affect its binding affinity to SmgGDS-607 (Fig. 7e), but abol-
ished the functions of Rab22a-NeoF1 in RhoA activation, cell 
migration, invasion and adhesion in both 143B and U2OS/MT300 
cells (Fig. 7f,g).
Next, we made the peptides cell permeable by conjugating them 
to the targeting peptide iRGD36,37. Peptide(WT)–iRGD (here-
after WT–iRGD), but not peptide(DD)–iRGD (hereafter DD–
iRGD), diminished the interaction between Rab22a-NeoF1 and 
SmgGDS-607 in cells (Fig. 7h) and decreased the level of RhoA–
GTP in ZOS-M cells that were positive for Rab22a-NeoF1 and in 
143B cells stably expressing Rab22a-NeoF1 (Fig. 7i,j). As both Itgav 
and Nrp1, which are required for the function of iRGD in cells, were 
detected in osteosarcoma cell lines including 143B, U2OS/MTX300, 
ZOS-M and ZOS (Extended Data Fig. 10b), we tested whether 
WT–iRGD attenuates the formation of lung metastases induced 
by Rab22a-NeoF1. First, WT–iRGD, but not DD–iRGD, inhib-
ited migration and invasion in a dose-dependent manner in both 
143B and U2OS/MTX300 cells stably expressing Rab22a-NeoF1 
(Extended Data Fig. 10c–f). Second, WT–iRGD, but not DD–iRGD, 
decreased cell migration, invasion and adhesion in both 143B and 
U2OS/MTX300 cells stably expressing Rab22a-NeoF1, but not 
cells stably expressing the empty vector (Fig. 7k–m). Similarly, 
WT–iRGD, but not DD–iRGD, decreased cell migration and invasion 
in both ZOS and ZOS-M cells carrying endogenous Rab22a-NeoF1 
(Extended Data Fig. 10g). Third, using 143B cells expressing fire-
fly luciferase (143B-Luc cells) and stably expressing Rab22a-NeoF1 
and ZOS-M cells expressing firefly luciferase (ZOS-M-Luc cells), we 
examined the effects of this iRGD fusion peptide on lung metas-
tasis in the in  vivo orthotopic model of osteosarcoma metasta-
sis, as illustrated in Extended Data Fig. 10h. WT–iRGD, but not 
DD–iRGD, inhibited lung metastases and increased the survival 
time of mice (Fig. 8a–f and Extended Data Fig. 10i–k). Collectively, 
these results demonstrate that perturbation of the interaction 
between Rab22a-NeoF1 and SmgGDS-607 using these targeting 
peptides based on Rab22a(WT) sequence abrogates lung metastasis 
induced by Rab22a-NeoF1, and suggest a promising strategy for a 
subset of patients with osteosarcoma and lung metastases.
Discussion
In this study, we have identified and validated recurring functional 
fusion proteins involving Rab22a1–38, which have a key role in driv-
ing tumour metastasis. Thus, any mutation or genomic alteration 
that leads to a truncation of Rab22a that includes Rab22a1–38 may 
result in expression of a metastasis-driving protein. Similar func-
tions of Rab22a truncations may occur with a frequency higher than 
the 5.4% that we observed in osteosarcoma with metastasis, as we 
only tested six fusion transcripts in our study.

drive lung metastasis in multiple cancer types. Residues 1–10 of 
Rab22a-NeoF1 conjugated to the cell-penetrating peptide iRGD 
prevented lung metastasis in the orthotopic osteosarcoma metas-
tasis model (Fig. 8a–f and Extended Data Fig. 10i–k), suggesting 
that this peptide may have potential as a treatment for patients with 
cancer who are positive for the Rab22a1–38 fusion gene(s).
The guanine nucleotide exchange factor SmgGDS-607 rec-
ognizes the polybasic region and the CAAX box of several small 
GTPases, and inhibits prenylation by impeding their entry into 
the geranylgeranylation pathway33,34,38,39. As illustrated in Fig. 8g, 
the newly synthesized RhoA-GTPase binds to both the nega-
tively charged region (red) via CAAX (black line) and the posi-
tively charged region (blue) of SmgGDS-607 until a signal triggers 
it to undergo GDP–GTP exchange and relocate to the plasma 
membrane via CAAX (black line)40. In osteosarcoma cells posi-
tive for Rab22a-NeoF1, Rab22a-NeoF1 binds to the negatively 
charged region of SmgGDS-607 via Arg4 and Lys7 to form a 
Rab22a-NeoF1–SmgGDS-607–RhoA complex, which may facili-
tate the communication between SmgGDS-607 and RhoA, thereby 
accelerating GDP–GTP exchange on RhoA. Consequently, more 
RhoA–GTP is transported to the plasma membrane, promoting 
lung metastasis. However, how Rab22a-NeoF1 facilitates the release 
of RhoA–GTP from the SmgGDS-607–RhoA complex needs fur-
ther investigation, as the association with SmgGDS-607 is neces-
sary but insufficient for Rab22a-NeoF1 to promote osteosarcoma 
lung metastasis (for example, in experiments with peptide(WT) and 
Rab22a-NeoF1(K18D)). One possibility is that Rab22a-NeoF1, as 
the product of an intron-fused transcript, exhibits distinct folding 
relevant to its activity as a competitor for RhoA–GTP. Our mecha-
nistic study of Rab22a-NeoF1 is an example of a gain of function 
caused by a fusion protein disrupting a protein–protein interaction 
(SmgGDS-607–RhoA), as proposed by Latysheva et al. 41.
In addition, our study suggests that genes formed by fusion of 
exons and introns, which have been excluded by most analyses, 
have the potential to result in fusion proteins that influence cancer 
progression. Thus, existing RNA-seq data may contain many fusion 
transcripts of interest that could be revealed using TopHat-fusion 
and SQUID algorithms that do not filter out exon–intron fusions.

Methods
Human samples. The study is compliant with all relevant ethical regulations 
regarding research involving human participants, and informed consent was 
obtained. The study design and use of fresh clinical osteosarcoma tissues and 
paraffin-embedded tissue samples were approved by the Institutional Review Board 
of Sun Yat-sen University. Tumour specimens collected from primary tumours of 
patients who underwent surgical resection at the Department of Musculoskeletal 
Oncology, First Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University, between 2014 and 2017, 
were reviewed by a pathologist and immediately frozen and stored at −80 °C until 
use (Supplementary Table 2). Tissue RNA extraction was performed using TRIzol 
according to the manufacturer’s instructions.
RNA-seq and whole-genome sequencing. RNA-seq and whole-genome 
sequencing (WGS) were performed by Novogene using an Illumina X TEN.  
The 6 GB and 90 GB clean data per sample were collected for RNA-seq and  
WGS, respectively.
Analysis of RNA-seq data and WGS. Hg38 assembly was used for the read 
alignment, and gene annotation was obtained using Ensembl gene annotation 
v.90. To search for fusion transcripts, we used the TopHat-2.1.042 Linux_x86_64 
version of the software. The following command was used to search for fusion 
transcripts: tophat-o OUTPUTDIRECTORY -p8 –fusion-search –keep-fasta-order 
–no-coverage-search -r0 –mate -std-dev 80 –max-intron-length 100000 –
fusion-min-dist 100000 –fusion-anchor-length13 –fusion-ignore-chromosomes 
chM BOWTIE2_INDEX sample_1.fastq.gz sample_2.fastq. gz. SQUID43 was used 
to derive the genomic structural variation from the RNA-seq data. The reads were 
aligned to the reference genome using STAR44. The chimeric transcripts were 
sorted and converted to BAM format using sambamba45. Structural variations were 
detected by SQUID using default parameters.
For WGS, reads were mapped to hg38 using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner 
(BWA) and sorted using PICARD (https://github.com/broadinstitute/picard). The 
structural variations were identified using the DELLY germline-calling method. 
Structural variations involving chromosome 20 were visualized using Circos.
To analyse RAB22A fusions from the CCLE dataset, raw RNA-seq data were 
downloaded from https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/ and processed by TopHat-fusion 
and SQUID. To identify RAB22A fusions in The Cancer Genome Atlas dataset, 
we searched the cBio portal (http://www.cbioportal.org) for all mutations related 
to RAB22A, and identified four tumour samples with RAB22A fusions. We then 
checked the break points of the four fusions to keep those that break immediately 
after exon 2 and downloaded the corresponding raw RNA-seq data for analysis 
with TopHat-fusion and SQUID.
Cell culture. Human cell lines U2OS, MG63, 143B, HOS, MNNG-HOS, SaOS2, 
BT-474, HCC2935 and HEK293T were cultured according to instructions from 
the American Type Culture Collection. ZOS, ZOS-M and U2OS/MTX300 cells 
were cultured as described previously23,25. The 143B-Luc and U2OS/MTX300-Luc 
cells were cultured as described previously46, and their derived stable cell lines were 
generated by infecting the cells with lentiviruses carrying the relevant shRNAs or 
cDNAs and carrying out selection with puromycin. All cell lines used in this study 
were authenticated using short-tandem-repeat profiling less than six months before 
the project was initiated, and were not cultured for more than one month.
Validation of fusion transcripts encoding Rab22a1–38. RAB22A-NeoF1–6, 
RAB22A-MYO9B and RAB22A-TEF fusion transcripts were identified by 
direct sequencing of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products spanning the 
breakpoint of the fusion genes. Among a cohort of 97 osteosarcoma specimens, the 
RAB22A-NeoF1–6 transcripts were screened by RT–PCR and Sanger sequencing. 
The primers used for RT–PCR are listed in Supplementary Table 6.
Transfection experiments. RAB22A-NeoF1 siRNAs were synthesized by 
GenePharma. Transfections were performed according to the manufacturer’s 
instructions using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent (Invitrogen) 
and 50 nmol l−1 siRNA. Transient transfection of 293T cells was performed with 
polyethyleneimine (25 kDa), and cells were collected after 48 h for subsequent assay 
and lentiviral packaging. The siRNAs used are listed in Supplementary Table 7.
RT–qPCR. Total RNA was prepared using TRIzol reagent, and cDNA was 
synthesized according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo). Quantitative 
PCR with reverse transcription (RT–qPCR) was performed using a Light Cycler 
480 instrument (Roche Diagnostics) with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Kapa). 
All reactions were carried out in a 10 μl reaction volume in triplicate. The primers 
for GAPDH were obtained from Invitrogen. Standard curves were generated and 
the relative amount of target-gene mRNA was normalized to that of GAPDH. 
Specificity was verified by melting curve analysis. The primers used for RT–qPCR 
are listed in Supplementary Table 6.
Plasmids, cloning, and lentivirus production. cDNAs for SmgGDS-607, 
SmgGDS-558, RhoA, RhoB, RhoC, Rac1 and Cdc42 were amplified by PCR and 
cloned into the pCDNA3.1 vector with a HA or Myc tag. RAB22A-NeoF1–6 
cDNAs were amplified by PCR and cloned into the C-SFB vector. The In-Fusion 
Cloning Kit (Clontech) was used to insert mutations to generate SmgGDS-
607(E254K/D256K), Rab22a-NeoF1(DD) and Rab22a-NeoF1(EE), and the 
sequence was verified. Lentiviruses were produced by co-transfection of plasmids 
psPAX2 and Pmd2.G into HEK293T cells as previously described46.
To generate RhoA- and SmgGDS-knockout human osteosarcoma cells with 
LentiCRISPR, guide RNA (gRNA) sequences were designed to target the coding 
sequence of RhoA and SmgGDS (http://crispr.mit.edu/). The used shRNAs and 
sgRNAs used are listed in Supplementary Table 8.
Cell viability assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2,000 cells 
per well. Cell viability was measured using MTT assays at 24 h.
Polyplex formation and nanoparticle delivery. Synthesis of the nonviral 
delivery reagent PEG-g-PEI was performed as described previously28. siRNA and 
PEG-g-PEI (N/P = 10; N/P: the ratio of the number of nitrogen atoms in delivery 
agents to that of the phosphorus atoms in siRNA) were mixed by gentle pipetting 
and kept at room temperature for 30 min to allow polyplex formation. The mixture 
was added to the cells for the indicated assay.
ZOS-M Luc and U2OS/MTX300-Luc cells were orthotopically implanted into 
nude mice after one week. The solution of siRNA (10 μM) and PEG-g-PEI polyplex 
was prepared at N/P = 10, mixed by gentle pipetting, and kept at room temperature 
for 30 min to allow polyplex formation. The mixture was then injected into the tail 
vein every other day (siRNA dose: 425 μg kg−1 body weight) for 4 weeks. Mice were 
monitored for lung metastases by quantitative bioluminescence imaging at the 
indicated time.
Generation of antibody RAD5-8 and hRAD5-8-v1-R5 antibodies against 
Rab22a-NeoF1. The monoclonal antibody RAD5-8 was generated using mouse 
hybridoma technology. In brief, a keyhole limpet haemocyanin-conjugated 
15-mer synthesized peptide, RAD5 (SunLight Peptides) derived from the reverse 
intron sequences of DOK5 in Rab22a-NeoF1 (68QQKSTTPASNHGKSR82) 
was used as the immunogen. For immunization of mice, the keyhole limpet 
haemocyanin-conjugated RAD5 peptide was emulsified in complete Freud’s 
adjuvant (Sigma–Aldrich) and injected into BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old, 
50 μg per dose). Immunizations were repeated with the same antigen using 
incomplete Freud’s adjuvant (Sigma–Aldrich) at two-week intervals for two more 
immunizations. Mouse sera were assayed for binding to the biotinylated synthetic 
RAD5 peptide (biotin–RAD5) by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). 
Mice with a sufficient antibody titre were given a final booster immunization and 
were euthanized 3 d later. Spleen cells from these mice were fused with myeloma 
Sp2/0 cells to generate hybridoma clones. ELISA was used to screen for biotin–
RAD5-specific monoclonal antibodies. After screening about 6,000 clones, 32 
clones that were specific for biotin–RAD5 peptide were selected. Among these, 
two monoclonal antibodies, RAD5-8 and RAD5-16, were specifically positive for 
Rab22a-NeoF1 (recombinant protein with a human Fc tag at the C-terminus). 
RAD5-8 had a higher binding affinity and was further validated for binding to the 
fusion protein by western blot.
For humanization RAD5-8, the variable (V) gene sequences of RAD5-8 were 
cloned from the hybridoma cells. The cloned V genes, including heavy-chain and 
light-chain V genes (VH and VL, respectively), were then cloned into expression 
vectors containing human constant regions of heavy- and light-chain, respectively, 
and co-transfected into 293F suspension cells to produce chimeric RAD5 
monoclonal antibodies. The chimeric RAD5 antibody was confirmed to bind to 
Rab22a-NeoF1 fusion protein with comparable binding activity to the parental 
mouse RAD5-8. Next, for humanization of the V genes of RAD5-8, combinatory 
approaches including complementarity-determining region grafting, framework 
shuffling and structure modelling were applied to design the first version of the 
humanized VH and VL genes. The designed humanized antibody, termed as 
hRAD5-8-v1, was confirmed to bind to Rab22a-NeoF1, and then underwent an 
affinity-maturation process to further improve its binding affinity using a phage 
display antibody library carrying mutations at the selected sites of the V genes. 
By library selection and ELISA screening, a final affinity-matured humanized 
monoclonal antibody, hRAD5-8-v1-R5, was identified. hRAD5-8-v1-R5 was 
expressed and purified by transient transfection of 293F cells, and used for  
western blotting.
Generation of anti-Rab22a-N antibody against the N terminus of 
Rab22a. Anti-Rab22a-N, an antibody against Rab22a1–38, was generated 
by immunizing rabbits with the synthetic peptide 1MALRLKVDl 
LGDTGVGKSSIVWRFVEDSFDPNINPT38I.
Boyden-chamber assays. Cell migration and invasion were examined using 
24-well Boyden chambers with 8 μm inserts, coated with (invasion) or without 
(migration) Matrigel, as previously described46. A total of 5 × 104 (osteosarcoma 
cell lines) and 3 × 105 (BT-474 or HCC2935 cells) per well were plated on the 
inserts and cultured at 37 °C in the top chambers without serum for 10 h and 
24 h, respectively. Cells that traversed the inserts were stained with crystal violet 
(0.005%, Sigma) and counted under phase-contrast microscopy.

Adhesion assay. Cell adherence to fibronectin was evaluated using culture plates 
that were pretreated with 10% BSA to block nonspecifically binding proteins and 
with fibronectin. The cells (5 × 105 cells per ml) were then suspended in serum-free 
medium at 37 °C and allowed to adhere to the plate for 30 min. Non-adherent  
cells were removed by gently washing three times with PBS, and the adhered  
cells were stained with crystal violet (0.005%, Sigma) and counted under 
phase-contrast microscopy.
Tandem affinity purification. TAP was carried out as described previously47. 
In brief, HEK293T cells were transfected with plasmids encoding C-terminal 
SFB-tagged Rab22a-NeoF1 and stable cells were selected with puromycin 
(1.0 μg ml). The cells were lysed in NETN buffer (100 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl 
(pH 8.0), 1 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM DTT, 0.5% Nonidet P-40) containing 50 mM 
β-glycerophosphate, 10 mM NaF and 1 mg ml−1 each of pepstatin A and aprotinin. 
The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm to remove debris and then incubated 
with streptavidin-conjugated beads (Amersham) for 4 h at 4 °C. The beads were 
washed five times with NETN buffer, followed by elution with NETN buffer 
containing 2 mg ml−1 biotin (Sigma). The eluates were incubated with S-protein 
beads (Novagen) for 4 h. After five washes, the bound proteins were analysed by 
SDS–PAGE, and mass spectrometry was performed by PTM BioLabs. The immune 
complexes were washed four times with NETN buffer and then analysed by SDS–
PAGE and western blotting.
Immunoblot and immunoprecipitation. For western blot analysis, cells were 
lysed in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-
40) containing protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Thermo 
Scientific). Lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 
4 °C. The cleared lysates were incubated with antibody beads or agarose overnight 
at 4 °C, or antibodies overnight at 4 °C followed by incubation with protein 
A/G PLUS-Agarose (Santa Cruz) at 4 °C for 2 h. Precipitates were then washed 
five times with cold RIPA buffer and eluted with 5× SDS sample buffer. The 
immunoprecipitates were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred to a PVDF 
membrane (Millipore). The membranes were blocked in TBS containing 5% 
non-fat milk and 0.1% Tween-20 and probed with primary antibodies overnight 
at 4 °C, washed 5 times with TBS containing 5% non-fat milk and 0.1% Tween-20, 
and then incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies 
(Promega). Clarity Western ECL substrate (Bio-Rad) was used for detection. The 
antibodies and their concentrations are listed in the Reporting Summary.
Protein expression and purification. SmgGDS-607 was amplified and cloned into 
a modified pET28 vector, and the correct constructs (confirmed by sequencing) 
were expressed in Escherichia coli Rosetta DE3 at 18 °C in terrific-broth medium 
supplemented with 0.1 mg ml−1 kanamycin and 34 μg ml−1 chloromycetin overnight 
after induction with 0.1 mM isopropyl-β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside when 
the optical density at 600 nm was 0.6–0.8. Cells were collected and lysed with 
buffer A (20 mM HEPES, 200 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, 1 mM PMSF, 2 mM 
β-mercaptoethanol, pH 7.5) using a cell disruptor (JNBIO) and centrifuged at 
18,000 rpm for 1 h. The supernatant was filtered and applied to a Ni-NTA column 
(GE Healthcare) equilibrated with buffer A. After washing with buffer B (buffer 
A with 30 mM imidazole), SmgGDS was eluted with buffer C (buffer A with 
300 mM imidazole). The eluted protein was further loaded onto a Superdex200 
16/60 column (GE Healthcare) equilibrated with buffer D (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM 
NaCl, 1 mM DTT) after His-tag cleavage by PreScission protease. The fractions 
were analysed by SDS–PAGE and stained with Coomassie blue. The target protein 
was collected, concentrated, aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until use.
Rho-activation assay. The standard Rho family-activity assay in cells was 
performed as a pull-down assay using glutathione S-transferase (GST)–Rhotekin–
Rho binding domain (RBD) protein beads and a monoclonal RhoA, RhoB or RhoC 
antibody, according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Cytoskeleton, RT02)48. ZOS-M 
cells or 143B cells stably expressing Rab22a-NeoF1 were treated with WT–iRGD or 
DD–iRGD for 24 h, and then immunoprecipitated with GST–RBD protein beads 
for 1 h followed by analysis by western blot.
For the RhoA–GTP assay in vitro, RhoA-knockout 143B cells were infected 
with Rab22a-NeoF1(WT)–SFB or Rab22a-NeoF1(DD)–SFB lentivirus and selected 
with puromycin (0.5 μg ml−1). The stable cell lysates were pulled down with 
streptavidin-coated beads (Sigma–Aldrich) overnight at 4 °C, then eluted with 
2 mg ml−1 biotin for 6 h at 4 °C, and Rab22a-NeoF1(WT) or Rab22a-NeoF1(DD) 
proteins were enriched in the eluates. 143B cell lysates (0.5 mg) were incubated 
with the eluates for 4 h at 4 °C, and then the peptide(WT) (10 μg) or peptide(DD) 
(10 μg) was added for 2 h at 4 °C, or 143B cell lysates (0.5 mg) were incubated with 
the synthetic Rab22a1–38(WT) (10 μg) or Rab22a1–38(DD) (10 μg) for 4 h at 4°C. 
Then the 143B cell lysates were immunoprecipitated with GST–RBD protein beads 
for 1 h followed by analysed by western blotting.
Fluorescence in situ hybridization. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue 
sections and cultured cell lines fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde were used for 
interphase FISH. All bacterial artificial chromosomes were obtained from the 
BACPAC Resources Center (Oakland, CA), and probe locations were verified by 
hybridization to metaphase spreads of normal peripheral lymphocytes. To detect 
the RAB22A-NeoF1 fusion gene, we used the following probes: RP11-10959 (Alexa 
Fluor 555-labelled; 5ʹ to RAB22A) and RP11-6L15 (Alexa Fluor 488-labelled; 3ʹ to 
DOK5). All samples were viewed on a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss).
Biotinylated peptide pull-down assay. All peptides were synthesized with an 
N-terminal biotin tag (Chinese Peptide). The assays were performed using  
30 μl of streptavidin-coated beads (Sigma–Aldrich) incubated with either 
different doses (2.5, 5 or 10 μM) of biotin-labelled peptides plus 1 mg of cell 
lysates, or different doses (0.25, 0.5, 1 or 2 mg ml−1) of cell lysates plus 5 μg of the 
biotin-labelled peptides. After 4 h incubation, the biotin precipitates were analysed 
by western blot.
iRGD block assay. Stable cells were treated with 10 μM of cell-penetrating WT–
iRGD and DD–iRGD for 24 h and then subjected to the migration, invasion and 
adhesion assay.
HEK293T cells were co-transfected with Rab22a-NeoF1–SFB and 
SmgGDS-607–HA, and then treated with 5 μg WT–iRGD and DD–iRGD for 24 h. 
Lysates were pulled down using anti-Flag beads and analysed by western blot.
Mouse xenografts. This study is compliant with all relevant ethical regulations 
regarding animal research. Animal experiments were approved by the Animal 
Research Committee of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center and performed in 
accordance with established guidelines. Four- to six-week-old male BALB/c nude 
mice were used for these experiments. For the in vivo orthotopic osteosarcoma 
metastasis model46,49,50, stable ZOS-Luc cells (2 × 106), ZOS-M-Luc cells (2 × 106), 
U2OS/MTX300-Luc cells (2 × 106) or 143B-Luc cells (6 × 105) were used per mouse 
and were injected into the distal femur and proximal tibia (six mice per group). For 
the tail-vein metastasis model, these cells were used at 5 × 106 per mouse (six mice 
per group). The mice were monitored for lung metastases using the IVIS Lumina 
Imaging System, and their body weights were monitored weekly. The animals were 
euthanized when their tumours reached 1.5 cm in diameter, and the lungs were 
collected and weighed.
For the iRGD block assay in vivo, 2 × 106 ZOS-M-Luc and 6 × 105 143B-Luc 
stable cells per mouse were injected into the distal femur and proximal tibia (6–8 
mice per group) for 10 d and 20 d, respectively, and then WT–iRGD or DD–iRGD 
(1 mg kg−1 or 10 mg kg−1) was injected into the tail vein every other day. The mice 
were monitored for lung metastases using the IVIS Lumina Imaging System, and 
their body weights were monitored weekly. After the treatments with iRGD peptides 
for four weeks, the mice were euthanized and the lungs were collected and weighed.
For the tail-vein metastasis model using TB-474 cells, female 4- to 6-week-old 
NOD/SCID mice were used. The in vivo breast cancer metastasis model used 
BT-474 stable cells at 7 × 106 cells per mouse; the cells were injected into the tail 
vein of mice (six mice per group). After two months, the mice were euthanized, 
and the lungs were collected and weighed. The number of metastatic nodules was 
determined microscopically in the lungs.
The number of metastatic nodules was determined microscopically in the 
lungs. Survival was calculated with a cut-off tumour volume of 1,500 mm3 using 
SPSS software, and statistical significance of the Kaplan–Meier analysis was 
determined using the long-rank Mantel–Cox) test.
Statistics and reproducibility. The mean values obtained for the control and 
experimental groups were analysed for significant differences. Each experiment 
was repeated independently with similar results. The data are presented as 
mean ± s.d. or mean ± s.e.m. Error bars s.d. or s.e.m. SPSS software (v.16.0, SPSS) 
was used for the statistical analysis. P values were obtained by two-tailed Student's 
t-test. Mouse survival was determined using Kaplan–Meier plots and compared 
using the long-rank test.
